\begin{abstract}
When does pretraining change an independent task's linear readability, and how large or persistent can that change be? Local second moments represent a convolution--square--pool encoder exactly for arbitrary input distributions; standard population regression connects tasks to subspace geometry. We separate exterior encoder updates caused by data/objective anisotropy from those caused by parameter mobility. In a solvable family with a genuine encoder bottleneck, an open task cone undergoes finite improvement followed by excess risk above initialization. A quantitative stability theorem retains both encoder and projection gaps under six independent initialization perturbations around one fixed configuration. Its small explicit neighborhood is controlled through angular imbalance, without a global physical-time perturbation bound. General task cross blocks and normal movement constrain risk amplitude. For stochastic updates, exact task contraction separates first-order fluctuation from second-order leakage, yielding conditional finite-prefix error budgets and abstention rules. On controlled textures, source-frozen predictions succeed early on independent future streams and deteriorate late; error decomposition does not uniquely attribute that failure to noise. Empirical intervals remain distinct from strict stochastic guarantees. Earlier interval-certified projection rebound and CIFAR/DTD boundary evidence are retained. The scope is task-conditioned population geometry, not random-initialization typicality, a universal learning-rate rule, or finite-sample probe generalization.
\end{abstract}
