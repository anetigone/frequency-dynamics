\begin{abstract}
Improving a pretraining objective need not improve the information retained for an independent task. We study this mismatch in a shallow convolutional model with squaring, global average pooling, and a linear projection. On periodic Gaussian images, its centered representation is exactly linear in standardized frequency powers. A computer-assisted theorem certifies that actual factorized gradient flow first improves the optimal unregularized population projection risk and subsequently exceeds its initial risk by a fixed amount, while the pretraining loss strictly decreases. An explicit matched-function construction identifies a layerwise learning-rate threshold for reversing the initial risk direction. We distinguish selection within a fixed encoder subspace from changes to that subspace: ordinary linear factorization preserves the encoder row space under an isotropic population objective, whereas the square parameterization need not. Ten paired seeds confirm this distinction in population experiments and substantial square-model subspace motion under complete empirical training; finite-data linear motion also has a reproducible component. Population-based intervention predictions are informative early in empirical training but deteriorate late. CIFAR-10 experiments provide a narrower diagnostic: better supervised rank-8 heads recover most of the gap to a fixed encoder's full linear probe, but unsupervised learning-rate ratios do not establish a stable benefit beyond training speed. The results characterize task-dependent selection and its limits, rather than a universal rule for choosing learning rates.
\end{abstract}
