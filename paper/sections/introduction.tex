\section{Introduction}
Pretraining compresses inputs without access to the downstream task. A representation can therefore improve its training objective while rotating away from a direction that a later task needs. Understanding this possibility requires more than a downstream learning curve: regularized probes, finite labels, changing output scale, and different initial representations can each produce apparent performance changes.

We isolate the effect of the training path. Our model is a single convolutional layer followed by squaring, global average pooling (GAP), and a linear projection. For periodic Gaussian inputs, its centered output is $z=Aq=WBq$, where $q$ contains independent standardized frequency powers. This reduction retains the training geometry: we update the convolutional parameters and projection, rather than replacing their optimization by direct optimization of $A$. The optimal unregularized population linear risk depends only on the row space of $A$, so output scaling and probe shrinkage cannot explain a change in this risk.

Our first result is a finite-time construction. On an explicit continuous initialization box, factorized gradient flow reaches two fixed relative-energy sections at which risk has respectively improved and risen above initialization. The loss decreases throughout. A separate matched-function construction gives an exact threshold at which the layerwise update ratio changes the initial risk direction. The threshold is specific to this construction; the fixed-gap theorem is independently certified and does not follow by joining local expansions.

The representation being evaluated matters. A compressed projection may lose task information even when an invertible encoder retains everything. In a genuine encoder bottleneck, $K<C<d$, we therefore measure both spaces. For an isotropic population objective $L(A)=F(AA^\top)$, ordinary linear factorization $A=WV$ changes $V$ only by invertible left multiplication over finite time. Its encoder row space remains fixed even while its projection selects different directions inside it. The square model has a different parameter-induced update and can change the encoder row space itself.

Experiments test these statements progressively. Population experiments use 540 trajectories but ten paired initialization seeds as the statistical units. Complete finite-data training then tests whether empirical optimization reaches similar geometric states. Repeated stochastic streams reveal that linear encoder motion also has a stable finite-data component. Task-conditioned intervention predictions work well early but approach a constant-sign baseline late. Finally, CIFAR-10 distinguishes available encoder information from information selected by an eight-dimensional head. This diagnostic supports the relevance of compression direction without establishing a useful unsupervised intervention rule on natural images.

The paper contributes a certified fixed-gap risk construction, an explicit matched-function direction threshold, and controlled tests separating encoder-space motion from projection-space selection. Neither the general sensitivity to layerwise initialization nor downstream deterioration itself is claimed as new. We also report the empirical failures that limit transferring the mechanism to stochastic training and natural images.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Initialization and optimization geometry.}
Kunin et al.~\cite{kunin2024rich} analyze how layer imbalance and learning rates change feature learning, including comparisons with the same initial product. Our scale--learning-rate equivalence belongs to this broader setting. Its role here is to connect the actual parameter metric to an explicit independent-task risk threshold, rather than to establish scale sensitivity for the first time.

\paragraph{Projection heads and feature selection.}
Gui et al.~\cite{gui2023projection} characterize expansion and shrinkage induced by projection heads with fixed encoders. Xue et al.~\cite{xue2024benefits} study layerwise feature weighting and report settings with post-projection early-stopping gains. Torralba and Weiss~\cite{torralba2026theory} analyze the same broad convolution--nonlinearity--GAP architecture using GUPA; their Appendix M discusses nonunique decompositions, initialization effects, and recognition performance falling while the loss improves. Our question concerns finite-time population risk along actual joint parameter training, with fixed risk gaps and explicit encoder/projection separation.

\paragraph{Nonlinearity and collapse.}
Jing et al.~\cite{jing2022collapse} analyze two-layer linear contrastive dynamics and dimensional collapse; Tian~\cite{tian2023nonlinearity} studies the role of nonlinearity in contrastive dynamics. Chaudhry~\cite{chaudhry2026geometry} develops a geometric account of projection heads. The Jacobian Gram matrix governing our effective updates is distinct from a pullback loss Hessian. Moreover, our scalar variance remains positive and our texture experiments retain their numerical ranks: changing a task direction is not, by itself, dimensional collapse. Our contribution is scoped to the stated construction and measured models, without an exhaustive priority claim over all equivalent factorizations.
